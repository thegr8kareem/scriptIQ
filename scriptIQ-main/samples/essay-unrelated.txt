Cocoa Farming and Climate Adaptation in the Western Region

Cocoa remains the backbone of Ghana's agricultural exports, yet the crop is increasingly threatened by shifting rainfall patterns and rising temperatures. Farmers in the Western Region report that the traditional planting calendar, passed down through generations, no longer aligns with the onset of the rainy season.

Adaptation strategies have begun to emerge. Some farmers now intercrop cocoa with plantain and timber species to provide shade and reduce heat stress on young trees. Others have adopted drought-tolerant hybrid seedlings distributed through government programmes, although uptake is slowed by cost and by uncertainty about long-term yields.

Extension services play a crucial role in spreading these practices, but officers are few and cover vast districts, so much knowledge still travels informally between neighbouring farms. Radio programmes in local languages have proven surprisingly effective at filling this gap.

Unless adaptation accelerates, projections suggest that suitable land for cocoa cultivation could shrink dramatically by mid-century, with serious consequences for rural livelihoods and national export revenue.
